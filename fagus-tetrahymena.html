Introduction

Background & Objective

Objective

Evaluate the effects of different concentrations of Fagus sylvatica leaf extract on the growth rate of Tetrahymena to determine whether beech leaf phytochemicals inhibit or promote unicellular eukaryotic proliferation in a concentration-dependent manner.

Beech trees produce a rich suite of secondary metabolites — polyphenols, tannins, flavonoids — that may interact with cellular growth machinery. Tetrahymena thermophila serves as a well-characterized model organism for toxicity and growth assays due to its rapid doubling time and sensitivity to environmental change.

🌿

Fagus sylvatica

European beech. Leaf extract prepared at a stock concentration of 3.6 mg/mL, then diluted to produce working concentrations of 0, 0.6, 1.2, and 1.8 mg/mL.

🔬

Tetrahymena

Ciliated single-celled eukaryote. Working stock prepared at 2 × 10⁴ cells/mL. Growth tracked via cell counting across multiple time points over three days.

Methods

Experimental Pipeline

Step 01

Fagus sylvatica leaf extract

Leaves processed to produce a concentrated stock at 3.6 mg/mL. Diluted to target concentrations before use.

Step 02

Tetrahymena stock dilution

Original Tetrahymena culture diluted to working stock of 2 × 10⁴ cells/mL in appropriate media.

Step 03

Concentrated stock prep

Final working solutions prepared by combining leaf extract at each target concentration with Tetrahymena media to a 10 mL total volume.

Step 04

Tetrahymena cell counting

Cell concentration measured at multiple time points across Days 1–3 using haemocytometry.

One-Way ANOVA Result

No statistically significant difference was found between treatment groups (p = 0.9209, α = 0.05). The null hypothesis — that leaf extract concentration has no effect on growth rate — cannot be rejected based on the current dataset.

Observed trend: Despite the non-significant ANOVA, higher concentrations of leaf extract were directionally associated with a reduced growth rate slope (1.53 → 1.42 → 1.49 → 1.46). This non-monotonic pattern and high within-group variability (visible in boxplot error bars) suggest that a larger sample size or higher extract concentrations might reveal a statistically detectable effect.
Discussion

Interpreting the pattern

What we found

  • Higher concentrations of leaf extract were associated with a reduced growth rate slope — a biologically plausible directional trend
  • The ANOVA p-value of 0.9209 indicates no statistically significant difference between groups at α = 0.05
  • Within-group variability across time-of-day sampling windows appears to be a major source of noise
  • The non-monotonic 1.2 mg/mL value suggests potential hormetic effects or pipetting variability

Why it matters

  • Plant secondary metabolites such as polyphenols and tannins are known to have cytostatic properties in model organisms
  • The concentration range tested (0–1.8 mg/mL) may fall below the threshold for detectable inhibition in Tetrahymena
  • Local precipitation bias at Hope — an important parallel: local measurements may miss basin-scale processes, just as local extract effects may miss whole-cell responses
  • The result is informative: it bounds the no-effect range and guides future study design
Conclusion

Summary & future directions

It's related — directionally

Higher concentrations of Fagus sylvatica leaf extract were associated with a lower growth rate slope in Tetrahymena, consistent with a mild inhibitory effect of beech phytochemicals on cell proliferation.

Not statistically significant

The one-way ANOVA returned p = 0.9209, far above the α = 0.05 threshold. The differences observed are within the range of natural biological variability given this sample size and concentration range.

Future Directions

  • Explore higher extraction concentrations (e.g., 3.6–10 mg/mL) to find the inhibitory threshold
  • Optimize extraction methods using multiphase extraction to isolate specific phytochemical fractions
  • Increase sample replication to reduce within-group variance and improve statistical power
  • Standardize sampling time points to reduce time-of-day noise in cell counts
  • Test specific beech phytochemicals (tannic acid, quercetin) in isolation to identify active compounds
