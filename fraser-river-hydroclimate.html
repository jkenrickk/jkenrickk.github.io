Daily Correlation, Lag, and Peak-Flow Timing Analysis at Hope, British Columbia — 2013 to 2024

Location Fraser River at Hope, BC
Period 2013 – 2024
Authors Kenrick · Kwon · Putra · Yu
SCROLL
Background

Why the Fraser River at Hope?

The Fraser River at Hope drains a vast mountain basin and is a snowmelt-dominated system with strong, predictable seasonal discharge patterns. Understanding what controls those patterns has direct implications for flood risk, water supply, salmon ecosystems, and climate-change adaptation.

  • Discharge affects flood risk, water supply, and downstream ecosystems
  • The Fraser drains an enormous highland basin where winter precipitation accumulates as snowpack
  • Rising spring temperatures trigger rapid basin-wide snowmelt, driving the annual freshet
  • Understanding these controls helps predict how a warming climate will alter peak-flow timing and volume

Temperature

Controls the timing and rate of snowmelt. Clear seasonal cycle — cold winters, warm summers — repeating consistently year to year.

Snow Water Equivalent (SWE)

Represents stored winter water at Mission Ridge. Builds through winter, peaks in spring, then collapses as melt releases the accumulated snowpack.

Precipitation

Highly variable over time with many low-precipitation days and occasional extreme events. No clear seasonal pattern at the local Hope Airport station.

Research Questions

Four questions driving the analysis

RQ 01

Is monthly discharge positively correlated with same-month precipitation at Hope?

RQ 02

Is monthly discharge positively correlated with monthly mean temperature?

RQ 03

Does a warmer spring (Mar–May) lead to an earlier annual peak discharge day-of-year?

RQ 04

Does a higher winter snowpack (max SWE) increase spring–summer discharge (Apr–Aug)?

Methods

Statistical approach

STEP 01

Align Daily Series

Standardized dates across all datasets, restricted variables to overlapping timestamps, and preserved missing values as NaN to maintain transparency.

STEP 02

Analyze Raw Data

Plotted daily time series to check for seasonality, outliers, and data gaps before performing any statistical tests.

STEP 03

Build Analysis Variables

Computed monthly mean discharge, monthly total precipitation, monthly mean temperature, and annual snowmelt metrics (peak day-of-year, winter max SWE).

STEP 04

Test Each Research Question

Applied Pearson correlations, p-values, linear fits, and lagged daily correlations spanning 0 to 14 days to systematically test each RQ.

Process-based annual tests: Peak discharge day-of-year vs spring mean temperature (Mar–May) and winter max SWE (Nov–Mar) vs spring–summer mean discharge (Apr–Aug) directly tested runoff timing and storage-release mechanisms. Monthly aggregation reduces daily noise, lag tests check short response delays, and annual metrics connect statistics to hydrologic mechanisms.
Results

What the data showed

Figure 5 · RQ1

Monthly Discharge vs Monthly Precipitation

r = −0.45

p = 1.36 × 10⁻⁸  ·  n = 144 months

A significant but negative relationship — this did not support the predicted positive same-month rainfall-runoff relationship. Wetter months are actually associated with lower discharge.

Interpretation: Same-month local precipitation at Hope is a weak proxy for basin-wide runoff generation. High-precipitation months are typically winter months when cold temperatures mean water is stored as snow, not released as runoff. Temperature better captures melt-season discharge behaviour.
Figure 6 · RQ2

Monthly Discharge vs Monthly Temperature

r = +0.63

p = 4.49 × 10⁻¹⁷  ·  n = 144 months

A strong, highly significant positive relationship. Warmer months consistently show much higher discharge, directly consistent with snowmelt control of the seasonal hydrograph.

Interpretation: Temperature is the dominant control on monthly discharge variability. As temperatures rise in spring and early summer, accumulated snowpack melts basin-wide, generating the large seasonal discharge pulse characteristic of the Fraser freshet. This mechanism overwhelms any local rainfall signal.
Figure 7 · Lag 0–14 d

Lagged Discharge vs Precipitation

Weak negative at all lags

r ≈ −0.13 to −0.14 across all 0–14 day lags

Local precipitation at Hope is not a strong short-lag predictor of discharge at the basin outlet at any time delay from 0 to 14 days.

Figure 8 · Lag 0–14 d

Lagged Discharge vs Temperature

Positive, strongest at lag 0

r ≈ 0.47–0.56 across lags; peaks at same-day

Temperature acted as a more immediate proxy for discharge variability than precipitation — confirming it captures the dominant melt-driven signal in this basin.

Takeaway: The Fraser outlet integrates snow storage, travel time, and basin-scale averaging across a vast mountain catchment. Local rainfall at a single station simply cannot explain short-term discharge changes at the basin scale.
Figure 9 · RQ3 · Annual

Peak Timing vs Spring Temperature (Mar–May)

r = −0.61

p = 0.0335

Warmer spring temperatures were significantly associated with an earlier peak discharge day-of-year — warmer springs advance the freshet, consistent with temperature-driven snowmelt accelerating the hydrograph.

Caveat: Annual tests use fewer data points (n ≈ 11 years) than monthly analyses, so the direction is informative but uncertainty is higher. The negative sign is physically consistent: warmer springs → earlier melt → freshet peak shifts to an earlier day of year. A 1°C warming corresponds to roughly 1–2 weeks of earlier peak timing.
Figure 10 · RQ4 · Annual

Winter Max SWE (Nov–Mar) vs Apr–Aug Discharge

r = +0.66

p = 0.0273

Years with a larger winter snowpack at Mission Ridge consistently produced higher spring–summer discharge. Antecedent SWE is the primary reservoir controlling seasonal runoff magnitude.

Mechanism: Deep winter snowpacks store water at elevation throughout the cold season. When spring arrives and temperatures cross the melt threshold, this stored water is released as runoff over weeks to months, setting the magnitude of the melt-season discharge pulse. Higher SWE → more stored water → higher Apr–Aug flows.
Discussion

The Fraser at Hope is a snowmelt-dominated system

Key Evidence

  • Annual discharge peaks coincide with spring–summer snowmelt (May–July), not with peak precipitation events
  • Temperature (r = 0.63) is a far stronger monthly predictor than precipitation (r = −0.45)
  • Wetter months are associated with lower discharge — opposite to a rainfall-runoff signal
  • Lagged correlations with precipitation remain weakly negative at all lags (0–14 days)

Why?

  • Precipitation at Hope Airport is local, but the Fraser drains a vast mountain basin spanning hundreds of kilometres
  • Winter precipitation falls as snow at elevation — stored as snowpack until spring temperatures rise
  • Rising spring temperatures trigger rapid snowmelt basin-wide, overwhelming any local rain signal at the gauge
  • The outlet integrates snow storage, melt rates, and travel time across the entire watershed

Broader Relevance

As British Columbia continues to warm, earlier and potentially lower peak flows will significantly impact hydroelectric power operations, salmon migration timing and spawning conditions, and agricultural water supply across the entire Fraser basin. These statistical relationships quantify the hydrologic sensitivity that managers and planners must contend with.

Conclusion

Three takeaways

Temperature > Precipitation

Monthly discharge is much more strongly related to temperature (r = 0.63) than to local precipitation (r = −0.45). Warmer months bring higher flows; wetter months counterintuitively bring lower flows — a clear signature of snowmelt dominance.

Spring Warmth Advances the Freshet

Warmer spring temperatures (Mar–May) are significantly linked to earlier annual peak discharge (r = −0.61, p = 0.034). Temperature-driven snowmelt advances the hydrograph by an estimated 1–2 weeks per degree of warming.

Snowpack Sets the Volume

Years with greater winter SWE at Mission Ridge produce higher spring–summer discharge (r = 0.66, p = 0.027). Antecedent snowpack is the primary reservoir controlling seasonal runoff magnitude in the Fraser watershed.
